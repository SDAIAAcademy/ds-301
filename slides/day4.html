Session 1

Clustered data

  • Identify the unit at which a predictor varies
  • Compute standard errors clustered by district
  • Explain why clustering widens some intervals and not others
01
The case

Do the 13 programme districts use less water? The naive answer is suspiciously sure

−7.8%
programme against other districts, at equal size, band and temperature
±0.6
points: the naive 95% interval from 40,000 bills

Pressure management is decided per district. How many pieces of evidence are there: 40,000 or 30?

The model is Day 1's consumption model on the log scale with a programme flag. Ask the room to guess how wide the honest interval is before showing the next slides.

Lab 4.1, step 1

Ask first where each predictor varies: within districts or only between them

PredictorVariesEvidence comes from

The function in step 1 checks whether a column takes one value in every district. In R: tapply(x, district, function(v) length(unique(v))).

Why the rows are not independent

Households in one district share pressure, network and neighbourhood

  • Each district has its own level of consumption the model does not know.
  • Every household in it carries the same deviation.
  • 9,423 bills from D17 repeat one district's deviation 9,423 times.

Design effect

With m bills per district and a share ρ of variance between districts, the variance grows by about 1 + (m − 1)ρ. With m ≈ 1,300 and ρ = 0.06, about 80.

The square root of 80 is about 9: an interval nine times as wide, before allowing for unequal district sizes, which make it worse. ρ comes from lab 4.2. The formula is Kish's design effect for cluster samples.

Lab 4.1, step 3

Clustered errors let residuals within a district be correlated in any way

fit = smf.ols(FORMULA, data=b).fit(
    cov_type="cluster",
    cov_kwds={"groups": pd.factorize(b["district"])[0]})
  • The estimates do not change; only their standard errors.
  • They assume districts independent of each other, nothing within them.
  • In R: sandwich::vcovCL(fit, cluster = ~district).

The sandwich sums the score contributions of each district before squaring them, so whatever correlation a district has is captured. It needs enough clusters: with 30 the errors are slightly too small; the stretch item's district bootstrap gives 0.0430 against 0.0435.

Lab 4.1, step 4

The programme's standard error grows 13.7 times; household size's does not grow at all

Clustered over naive standard errors. Lab 4.1.

Household size and band are compared within every district, so each district's own level cancels out of their comparisons. The programme can only be compared across districts, where the district levels do not cancel.

Lab 4.1, step 3

From "certainly about 8% less" to "somewhere between 15% less and no difference"

Naive

−8.3% to −7.2%. A planner would budget on it.

Clustered by district

−15.3% to +0.5%. A planner would ask for more districts or a trial.

What goes wrong. Reporting the naive interval for a district-level policy is the most common error in utility analyses: the precision of 40,000 rows for a question that 30 districts answer.

The generator's true programme value is −6% (−0.06 on the log scale): outside the naive interval, inside the clustered one.

Checkpoint

Which interval widens most?

A model of 50,000 students' scores in 80 schools has two predictors: hours of homework (per student) and a new curriculum (per school). You switch to errors clustered by school. What happens?

Both coefficients change, and both intervals widen
The curriculum's interval widens far more than homework's, and neither estimate changes
Homework's interval widens most, because it has more variation
Nothing much: with 50,000 students clustering does not matter
Why. The curriculum varies only between 80 schools, so its evidence is 80 schools; homework is compared within every school.

Answer: B. D is the misconception the day is built against: the number of rows is not the amount of evidence.

Session 2

Partial pooling

  • Fit a random-intercept model
  • Read the district variance and the share of variance between districts
  • Show how small districts shrink towards the average
02
The idea

Ignore districts, give each its own mean, or let them share: partial pooling

Complete pooling

One mean for all. Districts differ, so it is wrong for most of them.

No pooling

Each district its own mean. D13's rests on 40 bills: noisy.

Partial pooling

Each district its own level, drawn from a distribution of district levels whose spread is estimated.

This is the framing of Gelman and Hill, chapter 12, and of Regression and Other Stories. Ghaliya asked for each district's level: no pooling is noisy for small districts, complete pooling ignores her question.

Lab 4.2, step 1

A random intercept gives each district its own level, drawn from a normal distribution

mix = smf.mixedlm(FORMULA, data=b, groups=b["district"]).fit()
# R:  lmer(log_m3 ~ tariff_band * household_size + bs(temperature_c, 5)
#                  + pressure_managed + (1 | district), data = b)
  • Two variances: between districts (τ²) and between bills within one (σ²).
  • The programme's standard error now counts districts, as clustering did.

MixedLM fits by REML by default, like lmer. With 40,000 rows and 30 groups it takes a few seconds. It may print a note about the boundary of the parameter space; it does not apply here because τ is clearly above zero.

Lab 4.2, step 2

Only 6% of the variance is between districts, and it still changes everything for the programme

0.079
SD between districts, log scale (about ±8%)
0.301
SD between bills within a district
6.4%
share of variance between districts

The share τ²/(τ² + σ²) is the intraclass correlation. Small, and multiplied by over 1,000 bills per district in the design effect. The generator used 0.10 and 0.30.

Lab 4.2, step 3

Small districts move towards the average; large ones stay where their data put them

Raw district means and random-intercept estimates against bills per district. Lab 4.2.

The multiplier is τ²/(τ² + σ²/n): 0.73 for D13 with 40 bills, 0.80 for D30 with 60, above 0.99 for D17. The lab checks that every district's shrinkage matches it. Against the generator's true district effects, the shrunken estimates are slightly closer than the raw means.

Lab 4.2, step 4

Naive, clustered, mixed: both honest intervals are many times wider than the naive one

Programme districts against others, % difference with 95% intervals. Labs 4.1 and 4.2.

The mixed estimate also moves, from −7.8% to −10.3%: least squares weighs bills, so D17, a programme district with a quarter of all bills and a high level, pulls the programme estimate up; the mixed model weighs districts more evenly. The truth, −5.8%, is inside both honest intervals.

Choosing

Clustered errors fix the uncertainty; a mixed model also estimates the districts

Clustered errorsRandom intercept
Assumesdistricts independent; nothing withindistrict levels normal, residuals independent within
Giveshonest standard errors for the same estimatesestimates, standard errors and each district's level
Needsenough clusters (30 is about the minimum)enough clusters to estimate τ

Ghaliya asked for district levels for supply targets: the mixed model answers that. For a single comparison, clustered errors are simpler to explain. Either is defensible; the naive interval is not.

Checkpoint

Which district moves most?

In a random-intercept model, district A has 50 bills and a raw mean 0.12 above average; district B has 5,000 bills and the same raw mean. What do their estimated levels look like?

Both shrink by the same share, as their raw means are equal
B shrinks more, because it has more bills to average
Neither moves: each district keeps its own mean
A moves noticeably towards the average; B stays almost at 0.12
Why. The shrinkage factor τ²/(τ² + σ²/n) is close to 1 for large n; a mean from 50 bills is less reliable, so it borrows more from the other districts.

Answer: D. Link to the plot: D13 and D30 against D17.

Session 3

Penalised regression

  • Screen candidate predictors for necessity before modelling
  • Tune ridge and lasso penalties by cross-validation
  • Measure how stable the lasso's selection is
03
The case

Billing exported 126 candidate columns to predict August consumption

Useful-looking

12 months of past consumption, property details, appliance answers, area climate.

Noise

38 survey questions, app logins, bill queries.

Nobody asked for

A member's chronic condition, nationality, income band, employer, phone brand, marital status.

Nidal Al-Fadhli exported everything the billing system holds for 2,000 households. The purpose of the forecast is next summer's supply plan.

Lab 4.3, step 1

Remove what the purpose cannot justify, before you check whether it predicts

  • The PDPL limits personal data to the minimum needed for the purpose (Article 11).
  • Health data is sensitive data under the Law.
  • A supply forecast needs none of the six columns.
What goes wrong. "Let the lasso decide." A penalty chooses by prediction; minimisation is decided by the purpose. A column that predicts can still be one you may not use.

Tamadur Al-Hussaini, the DPO, signs off the column list. The national AI ethics principles add fairness: nationality and income in a supply model would steer supply by group. In the lab the check passes only when exactly the six are removed, decided from names and purpose.

Lab 4.3, step 2

Ridge shrinks every coefficient a little and keeps them all

  • Least squares plus a penalty on the sum of squared coefficients.
  • Correlated predictors share the weight between them.
  • Standardise first, so one penalty treats all alike.
120
nonzero coefficients at the cross-validated penalty, 34.6

RidgeCV uses efficient leave-one-out cross-validation by default. In R: glmnet with alpha = 0. Ridge predicts well with correlated predictors and does not select.

Lab 4.3, step 3

The lasso sets coefficients to zero: one fit selects 27 of 120 predictors

  • A penalty on the sum of absolute coefficients.
  • Cross-validation picks the penalty that predicts best.
  • That penalty is chosen for prediction, not to find the true predictors.

Correlated groups

The twelve lag months are correlated at about 0.9. The lasso picks some of a group and drops the rest, almost at random.

LassoCV with 5 folds. In R: cv.glmnet with alpha = 1. The generator uses 8 predictors; one lasso fit selects 27, so about 19 are noise that helped the prediction slightly in this sample.

Lab 4.3, step 4

Over 100 bootstrap samples, eight predictors are selected every time; the rest come and go

Share of 100 bootstrap samples in which the lasso selects each predictor, 40 most frequent. Lab 4.3.

The eight: June, July and August of last year, garden area, pool, AC units, irrigation timer, area summer maximum. All eight are the generator's true terms. lag_m10 is selected in 94% of samples and is noise: a high frequency alone is not proof.

Lab 4.3, step 5

Report the stable set for prediction; report no selected predictor as a "driver"

Defensible

"Eight predictors were selected in every bootstrap sample; the forecast uses the lasso's predictions."

Not defensible

"The lasso shows that October consumption and dishwashers drive August demand."

Estimating after selecting. Least squares on the data that chose the predictors overstates them; select on one half and estimate on the other (the stretch item).

The stretch item finds the same eight with intervals excluding zero on the held-out half. That is a sample split, the simplest honest approach to inference after selection.

Checkpoint

What do you tell the planner?

A lasso with a cross-validated penalty selected 25 of 90 predictors. Over 100 bootstrap samples, 6 are selected every time and 40 are selected between 20% and 80% of the time. What can you say?

The 25 selected predictors are the drivers of demand
The 6 are a stable core; the others' selection depends on the sample and should not be reported as findings
The model is useless and should be dropped
Lower the penalty until all 40 are always selected
Why. Selection is itself uncertain; only what survives resampling is a stable choice, and even that is a predictive, not a causal, statement.

Answer: B.

Lab 4.4, steps 1 and 4

The use decides which words you may write

Descriptive

"used", "differed", "at equal size and band": the consumption report.

Predictive

"forecast", "expected", "with this error": the August forecast.

Causal

"reduces", "the effect of": only with a randomised or well-argued comparison, as in brief C2.

This closes the loop of Day 1's three uses. The stretch item of lab 4.4 searches the page for causal words; a neighbour finds the subtler ones.

Lab 4.4, step 4

Limits say what the numbers are not, what the intervals leave out, and where not to use the model

  • The programme figure compares districts that chose to join; it is not the saving from joining.
  • Intervals cover sampling and district variation in 2026, not a change of tariff.
  • Temperatures above 43 °C and a new district without history are outside the data.

Assessors in the capstone defence ask: "where would your model be wrong?" A limits section written in advance is the answer.

Day 4 · wrap-up

Three things to keep

Count the evidence

A district-level predictor has 30 districts behind it, not 40,000 bills.

Purpose before penalty

Minimise first; then trust only the selections that survive resampling.

Five parts

Question, use and estimand, model and checks, effects, limits.

Ask each participant to say which brief they chose and its use in one sentence.
