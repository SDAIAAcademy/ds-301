Lab 3.1, step 2

Raw residuals of a yes-or-no model make two bands, whatever the model

  • A late bill leaves 1 − p; an on-time bill leaves −p.
  • Plotted against anything, they form two curved bands.
  • Information appears only in averages of many residuals.

Binned residuals

Sort by a predictor, cut into 20 bins, plot each bin's mean residual with a ±2 standard-error band. About one bin in 20 falls outside when the form is right.

The binned residual plot is from Gelman and Hill, and appears in Regression and Other Stories. The band is 2·√(p̄(1 − p̄)/n) for each bin. U.binned_residuals computes it; a predictor with few values gets one bin per value.

Lab 3.1, steps 3 and 4

Against the bill, 7 of 20 bins fall outside the band; with its log, none

Mean residual in 20 bins of 2,000 bills. Lab 3.1.
  • Small bills: late less often than the model says (−5.3 points).
  • Middle bills: more often (+2 to +3 points).
  • The largest bills: less often again.
  • AIC falls from 35,947 to 35,798 with the log.

Against household size no bin falls outside, against past late payments one: what chance gives. The pattern against the bill is the shape of a log: the burden of a bill grows fast at first and slowly later. The generator uses log(bill/100); the repaired coefficient is 0.85 against 0.90 planted.

The idea

If the model is right, data simulated from it look like the data you have

→

→

→
4 · Comparewith the observed value
Day 1 in reverse. Fake data checked the method; these replicates check the model against the data.

This is a posterior predictive check in its simplest form, as in Regression and Other Stories, here without Bayesian machinery: the fitted coefficients are taken as known (the stretch item adds their uncertainty).

Lab 3.3, steps 2 to 4

No Poisson replicate has as many zero-leak segments as Sahil; the negative binomial does

Share of segments with no leak in 1,000 replicate years from each fit. Lab 3.3.

Poisson replicates: 23.3% to 31.0%; observed 36.4%; tail share 0. Negative binomial: 32.6% to 42.3%, tail share 0.48. The largest count tells the same story: Poisson replicates top out at 62, the observed is 75; the negative binomial's median is 64.

Lab 3.3, step 3

A negative binomial count is a Poisson count with a gamma-distributed rate

def replicate_nb(mu, alpha, R, gen):
    lam = mu * gen.gamma(1 / alpha, alpha, size=(R, len(mu)))   # mean 1, variance alpha
    return gen.poisson(lam)

Each replicate gives each segment its own hidden leakiness: exactly what the Poisson model leaves out.

This is also how the generator built Sahil's leaks: a gamma frailty with shape 1.5 per segment. In R: rnbinom(n, size = 1/alpha, mu = mu).

The next model

Reach for a zero-inflated model only when the simpler one fails the check

  • Excess zeros against a Poisson model are often just overdispersion.
  • Here the negative binomial reproduces both the zeros and the largest count.
  • A zero-inflated model would need its own story: mains that cannot leak at all.

Zero-inflated and hurdle models are outside this course's entry; mention them by name only. The check of this session is how you would know you needed one.
