Generalised linear models

The outcome chooses the family; the link connects its mean to a straight line

OutcomeFamilyLinkA coefficient is

Straight on the link scale; curved, and bounded, on the outcome's scale.

Write the logistic model on the board: log(p / (1 − p)) = β₀ + β·x, so p = 1 / (1 + e^(−η)). The point to stress: the family is chosen from what the outcome is (a flag, a count), before looking at any fit. In R: glm(family = binomial).

Lab 2.1, step 5

By decile, the logistic model sits on the diagonal; the line misses at both ends

Mean prediction and observed rate in ten groups of 4,000 bills.
  • Lowest decile: the line predicts −4.1%; 2.8% are late.
  • Largest gap: 6.9 points for the line, 2.4 for the logistic model.

This is a calibration plot, also seen in DS-204; here it is a model check, not a model-selection device. The stretch item shows that the logistic model's average autopay difference is also −25 points: the line gets the average right and the individual bills wrong.

A fair hearing

A linear probability model can describe one average difference, not probabilities

Acceptable

Predictions stay inside 0.1 to 0.9, and the question is one average difference, read as points.

Not acceptable

Predicting a bill's probability, ranking households near the bounds, or any rare outcome.

Here. Its −25 points matches the logistic average, −25.2. It is the bills, not the average, that the line gets wrong.

Some economists prefer the line for its simple reading. That is a fair position for an average effect in a randomised design, with robust errors. For collections planning, Nidal needs bill-level probabilities, and the line produces impossible ones.

Reading a logistic model

02
Lab 2.2, step 1

exp(coefficient) is an odds ratio: exact, and hard to use

TermOdds ratio95% interval
autopay0.1000.092 to 0.110
each past late payment1.591.54 to 1.64
each extra person1.061.05 to 1.08
each extra SAR on the bill1.0031.003 to 1.004
What goes wrong. "Autopay cuts late payment by 90%." It multiplies the odds by 0.1; the probability falls by much less, and by a different amount for each household.

Odds = p / (1 − p). At p = 0.5 the odds are 1; a tenth of that is 0.1, so p ≈ 0.09. At p = 0.05 the odds are 0.053; a tenth is 0.005, so p ≈ 0.005. Same odds ratio, a 41-point drop and a 4.5-point drop.

Lab 2.2, step 5

Households chose autopay, so the difference describes them, not what promoting it would do

Write

"Among households like A, bills on autopay were late about 48 points less often than bills without it (46 to 49)."

Do not write

"Autopay would cut A's late payments by 48 points."

To learn the effect. Offer autopay enrolment to a random half of A-like households and compare.

The lab checks each sentence for the words reduces, cuts, causes, effect and would. People who enrol in autopay may be more organised, or better off: the model cannot see that. A trial answers Nidal's real question; DS-205 covers designing one.

Lab 2.3, step 3

The offset puts log exposure in the model with its coefficient fixed at 1

po = smf.glm("leaks ~ material + age_years + pressure_bar", data=s,
             family=sm.families.Poisson(),
             offset=np.log(s["km_years"])).fit()

log E[leaks] = log(km-years) + β₀ + β·x, so the β describe the rate per kilometre-year.

In R: glm(leaks ~ material + age + pressure + offset(log(km_years)), family = poisson). In the stretch item, log length as a free predictor gets 1.10 (1.05 to 1.15) on full-year segments: close to 1, and its Poisson interval is too narrow, which session 4 explains.

Checkpoint

What does the offset do?

A colleague fits leak counts on material and age with a Poisson model and no offset. Segments of asbestos cement happen to be shorter on average. What happens to the asbestos-cement coefficient?

Nothing: a Poisson model accounts for length
It compares counts, not rates, so short asbestos-cement mains look less leaky than they are
Its standard error grows, but the estimate is fine
Only predictions change; coefficients are unaffected
Why. Without the exposure, a coefficient describes counts per segment; shorter segments have fewer chances to leak, which hides a higher rate.

Answer: B. Then ask what an alternative to the offset is: log length as a predictor, whose coefficient should come out near 1 (the stretch item).

Session 4

Overdispersion

  • Measure dispersion from a fitted Poisson model
  • Fit a negative binomial model with the same exposure
  • Compare effects and intervals on the count scale
04
An assumption you can check

A Poisson model promises that a count's variance equals its mean

  • Mains of the same material and age still differ: soil, workmanship, traffic.
  • That hidden leakiness adds variance the model does not know about.
  • The coefficients survive; the standard errors are too small.

This is the first of the five problems in Qusai's models that Day 3 logs: overdispersion. Ask: if every main had its own hidden rate, what would the counts look like? Many zeros and a few mains with many leaks.

Lab 2.4, steps 1 and 2

In every group the variance is above the mean; overall about 2.3 times

Ten groups of 120 segments by fitted mean. Lab 2.4.
  • Pearson dispersion: 2.32; about 1 if the Poisson model fitted.
  • The top group: mean 10.7, variance 153.
  • The curve mean + α·mean² follows the points.

Dispersion = sum of squared Pearson residuals / residual degrees of freedom. Values well above 1 mean overdispersion. Below 1, underdispersion, is rare in counts. In R: sum(residuals(fit, "pearson")^2) / df.residual(fit).

Lab 2.4, step 3

The negative binomial adds one parameter for the extra variance

nb = smf.negativebinomial(
    "leaks ~ material + age_years + pressure_bar",
    data=s, exposure=s["km_years"]).fit(disp=0)
PoissonNeg. binomial
α00.60 (0.50 to 0.71)
AIC4,9684,240

variance = μ + α·μ². With α = 0 it is the Poisson model.

The generator gave each segment a gamma-distributed leakiness with shape 1.5, which is exactly a negative binomial with α = 0.67; the fit recovers 0.60. In R: MASS::glm.nb with offset(log(km_years)).

Lab 2.4, step 4

The rate ratios barely move; their intervals widen by 1.6 to 1.8 times

Rate ratios with 95% intervals: Poisson in coral, negative binomial in teal. Lab 2.4.

Ductile iron: the Poisson interval 0.98 to 1.22 almost excludes 1; the negative binomial's 0.97 to 1.37 is far from settling it. A renewal list ranked by Poisson intervals would treat that difference as known.

Checkpoint

What does a dispersion of 2.3 change?

A Poisson model of leak counts has a Pearson dispersion of 2.3. Its rate ratio for asbestos cement is 1.62 with a 95% interval of 1.38 to 1.91. What should Basil expect from a negative binomial refit?

A much smaller rate ratio, since the Poisson estimate is inflated
The same interval, with a better AIC
A similar rate ratio with an interval about one and a half times as wide
No answer: counts with overdispersion need a zero-inflated model
Why. Both models describe the same mean; the negative binomial admits the extra variance, which widens the intervals by about the square root of the dispersion.

Answer: C. The square root of 2.3 is 1.52; the refit widened them 1.6 to 1.8 times. D is a reasonable next question, which lab 3.3 tests by simulation.

Day 2 · wrap-up

Three things to keep

Family from the outcome

A flag is binomial, a count is Poisson or negative binomial, before any fit.

Read on the reader's scale

Probability differences at stated profiles; expected counts with their exposure.

Check the variance

A dispersion well above 1 means the Poisson intervals are too narrow.

Ask for one sentence each: the autopay difference for profile B, without causal words.
