01
The case

Every regression Ghaliya receives answers one of three questions

Describe

How does consumption differ between tariff bands, among the households we bill?

Predict

How much will each district draw next August?

Explain a cause

Would a usage letter make households use less?

Sahil Water. A fictional utility: 40,000 households in 30 districts, one month's bill each, 1,200 pipe segments with weekly leak reports. All synthetic, seed 301.

Ask the room to name one regression they produced this year and say which of the three it was. Most will say "predict" or "describe"; many will admit a report read it as cause. That gap is the session.

The data: bills.csv, segments.csv, leaks.csv and two files from Qusai Al-Mufarrij, the analyst who left. His models carry the five problems of Day 3.

This is the course's responsible-use rule in one slide: no coefficient is presented as a tariff's effect on poorer households. The band is an eligibility category tied to income; a comparison across bands describes, it does not explain.

Before any model

Write the estimand first: the quantity, in whom, compared with what

1 · QuestionHow much more water does one more person mean?
→
2 · EstimandAverage difference in m³ a month, billed households, by band
→
3 · ModelLog consumption with size, band, a temperature spline
→
4 · EstimateA number with an interval, in the estimand's units
Takeaway. The estimand decides the model and what its output must be turned into. Lab 1.4 answers exactly this one.

Press once per step. At step 2, stress three parts: the population (billed households this month), the outcome with its unit (m³ a month), the comparison (one more person, within band). At step 4: a log-scale coefficient is not yet an estimate of this estimand; it has to be turned into m³.

The evidence each use needs

Each use asks something different of the data

Descriptive

A fair sample of the stated population. Adjusting for a variable changes the comparison, not its meaning.

Predictive

Predictions checked on cases the model did not see. Coefficients need no reading at all.

Causal

A design that makes compared groups alike apart from the action: random assignment, or strong stated assumptions.

A good fit supports none of them on its own.

Common confusion: "we controlled for size and temperature, so it is causal now." Adjustment narrows the comparison; it does not create a design. Ask the room what would have to be true for the social-tariff coefficient to be causal: households would have to be on the tariff for reasons unrelated to how they use water. They are not.

The national rules

A planning model built on billing data is a secondary use of that data

  • The General Rules for Secondary Use of Data define it as using data for purposes other than those of collection.
  • The Personal Data Protection Law asks that personal data be limited to the minimum necessary for the purpose.
  • So every model of this course states its purpose, and lab 4.3 removes the columns that purpose cannot justify.
  • Households appear as pseudonymised keys; results are reported by band or district only.

Sources: General Rules for Secondary Use of Data, section "First: Definitions", item 1; Personal Data Protection Law, Article 11, paragraph 3. Both are on sdaia.gov.sa; addresses are in the README. Do not paraphrase beyond these two points: we quote what we read.

What you bring from DS-201

You can already fit a line, put an interval on its slope and spot confounding

  • Least squares and the slope in the reader's units
  • A slope interval, and a robust one when residuals fan out
  • Adding a variable moves a slope when the two travel together

New here

Curves and interactions, families other than the normal, checks by simulation, grouped data and many predictors.

One slide of recap: DS-201 Day 4 did all three on the Zulal case. Nothing of it is refitted from scratch. If several participants did not take DS-201, give them the pre-work notebook P0 tonight.

Checkpoint

Which use does this evidence support?

A vendor's late-payment model, trained on 2025 bills, ranks 2026 bills well: 80% of the late payers are in its top fifth. The vendor says sending reminders to that fifth will cut late payment. What does the evidence support?

That reminders to the top fifth will cut late payment
That the model predicts which 2026 bills will be paid late
That the model's variables explain why bills are paid late
Nothing, since the model was not tested in a trial
Why. The model was checked on bills it did not see, which supports prediction. What a reminder changes is a causal question that needs reminders to be sent, ideally at random.

Answer: B. If many choose A, return to the table of what each use needs. If D, point out that a trial is what the causal claim needs, not the predictive one.

Session 2

Fake data first

  • Simulate an outcome from a stated model with a seed
  • Recover known coefficients and check their intervals by coverage

02
The problem with real data

On real data you never learn whether the fit got the answer right

  • A fit always returns numbers, with intervals, whatever the code does.
  • Fake data reverse it: you choose the coefficients, generate outcomes, fit, and compare.
  • A miss means the code, the model or the design is wrong.

In lab 1.2

The 40,000 real households keep their size, band and temperature; only their consumption is invented from a model you write.

The habit comes from Gelman, Hill and Vehtari's workflow (Regression and Other Stories): simulate from the model before fitting it to data. Ask: when did your last model's code get checked against a known answer? Usually never.

Lab 1.2, step 1

A simulator is the model written forwards

TRUE = {"Intercept": 2.00, "household_size": 0.095, "social": -0.45,
        "villa": 0.40, "temperature_c": 0.030, "sigma": 0.30}

def simulate(X, p, gen):
    shift = np.select([X.tariff_band == "social", X.tariff_band == "villa"],
                      [p["social"], p["villa"]], 0.0)
    mean = (p["Intercept"] + p["household_size"] * X.household_size
            + shift + p["temperature_c"] * X.temperature_c)
    return mean + gen.normal(0, p["sigma"], len(X))

Read it line by line: the mean on the log scale, then normal noise. Every assumption of the fitted model is visible here, which is the point. The social shift is deliberately strong in this fake world so that step 4 shows a clear bias.

Lab 1.2, step 3

Over 200 fake data sets, 94% of the intervals hold the planted value

200 estimates of the size coefficient; the planted value in coral.
  • The spread of the 200 estimates, 0.00083, matches one fit's standard error, 0.00079.
  • 94% is within simulation noise of 95%.
  • Coverage is a claim you can test, not a property you trust.

With 200 repeats, coverage has a simulation error of about 1.5 points, so 94% is fine; 85% would not be. The stretch item makes the error spread grow with size: the classical interval then covers less, and HC3 restores it. Day 3 returns to that funnel.

Why. Social-tariff households are larger (5.5 people against 4.2 on standard) and, in this fake world, use much less. Without the band, size absorbs part of the band's shift.

The short model's interval is narrow and excludes the truth. Nothing in its summary tells you. This is the DS-201 confounding lesson with one twist: here you know the answer, so you can see it. Ask what other term in a real consumption model could play the band's role: district, housing type.

The limit

Fake data test the method, never the world

They show

The code is right; the estimator recovers what it should; intervals cover; a term left out biases what you expect.

They cannot show

That the real bills follow your model. That comes from checking the fit against the data: Day 3.

Habit. Before any new model in the capstone, simulate from it once and fit it.

Tie forward: Day 3 session 3 simulates from the fitted model to check it against the data. Same tool, the other direction.

Checkpoint

What does a perfect recovery prove?

You simulate bills from a model with a temperature spline, fit the same model and recover every coefficient within its interval; coverage over 200 repeats is 95%. What have you shown?

That Sahil's real bills follow a temperature spline
That the temperature effect is causal
That the real model's intervals will cover 95%
That the code and the estimator work when the model is right
Why. You planted the model, so recovering it checks the fitting machinery only. Whether real data follow it is a separate check against the data.

Answer: D. C is the subtle one: real errors can be clustered by district (Day 4) or fan out (Day 3), and then coverage falls.

Session 3

Transformations and splines

  • Fit a model of log consumption and read its coefficients as percentages
  • Replace a straight temperature term with a spline and compare the fits
  • Plot a fitted curve with its band and back-transform it correctly
03
Transformations

On the log scale, a coefficient is a percentage change

  • Consumption is skewed, and a hot month adds a share, not a fixed amount.
  • A coefficient b on log consumption means about 100 × (eb − 1)% per unit.
  • The straight temperature term in lab 1.3: 3.0% more per degree, at every temperature.

Rule of thumb

For b below 0.1, the percentage is close to 100 × b. For 0.40 it is 49%, not 40%.

Do the villa band shift from the generator aloud: 0.40 on the log scale is e to the 0.40, 1.49, so 49% more. "40%" is a common slip. Every percentage on today's slides is computed this way.

Lab 1.3, step 2

A straight temperature term is too high in mild months and too low in hot ones

Mean residual in each two-degree bin, 40,000 bills. Lab 1.3 solution run.

The line's residuals run from +0.13 in the coolest bin through -0.10 in the middle to +0.26 in the hottest: a U. On the log scale 0.26 is about 30% too little water predicted for the hottest months, which is when supply runs short. The spline's residuals sit within 0.03 of zero everywhere.

A flexible term

A spline lets the data choose the curve; you choose only how flexible

  • Polynomial pieces joined smoothly at knots.
  • bs(temperature_c, df=5): five columns; the model weights them.
  • More degrees of freedom bend more; cross-validation can pick (the stretch item).
  • Its coefficients mean nothing alone: read the curve.
smf.ols("log_m3 ~ household_size + tariff_band"
        " + bs(temperature_c, df=5)", data=b).fit()
Outside the data. A spline says nothing beyond 16 to 42 °C. Predictions there need a stated reason.

AIC fell from about 23,570 to about 21,070, but the residual plot is the better argument for planners. In the stretch, 3, 5 and 8 degrees of freedom give nearly the same held-out error; any of them beats the line. In R the same term is splines::bs or mgcv::s.

The band comes from 1,000 draws of the coefficients (U.predict_band). It widens at 40 °C because fewer bills are that hot. The coral dashed line is the straight model on the same scale: it says too much in mild months and far too little in the hottest. That difference is a supply plan.

Reading the band

The band is for the mean, not for one household

Interval for the mean

Where the average household of this kind sits: 37.3 to 38.4 m³ at 40 °C. Narrow, because 40,000 bills pin it down.

Interval for one household

Where one such household's bill may fall: many times wider, since σ is about 0.31 on the log scale.

Use. Supply planning adds households up: the mean band. A single customer's complaint about a high bill: the household interval.

Ask the room which interval a planner sizing a reservoir needs (the mean, summed) and which a call-centre agent needs (the household). People report the narrow band when they mean the wide one.

Lab 1.3, step 5

exp(prediction) is the median; the mean is about 5% higher

19.7 m³

20.7 m³

20.4 m³

What goes wrong. A plan that sums exp(prediction) over households runs about 5% short, every month.

The observed median of the same 435 bills is 19.5: exp(prediction) is the median, as the theory says. The mean back-transform assumes normal errors on the log scale; with skewed residuals use the smearing estimate (the mean of exp(residuals)) instead.

Checkpoint

What did the planner's total get wrong?

A planner fits log consumption with a temperature spline, takes exp of each household's prediction for August and sums them to size the district's supply. The total comes out below the meters' August total. What is the most likely reason?

exp of a log-scale prediction is a median, which sits below the mean
The spline has too few degrees of freedom to fit August
The interval for the mean was too narrow
Log models cannot be used for volumes
Why. The mean of the log is below the log of the mean, so exp(prediction) underestimates the mean. Add σ²/2 before exp, or use smearing.

Session 4

Interactions and marginal effects

  • Add an interaction and say what each of its coefficients means
  • Compute an average marginal effect on the outcome scale
  • Report marginal effects by group with intervals from simulation
04
Lab 1.4, steps 1 and 2

An interaction gives each band its own slope for household size

"log_m3 ~ tariff_band * household_size
          + bs(temperature_c, df=5)"
BandSlope, log scalePer person
standard0.095610.0%
social0.0956 − 0.02197.6%
villa0.0956 + 0.018712.1%
What goes wrong. Reading −0.0219 as "the effect of size on social households". It is the difference between the social and the standard slopes.

The star expands to both main terms and their product. The reference band is standard, so its slope is the main term; the others add their interaction term. Ask: what does the social main effect mean now? The social-standard difference for a household of size zero, which does not exist. Main effects in an interaction model rarely mean anything alone.

The question was in cubic metres

A percentage per person is not what Ghaliya asked

  • 12.1% of a villa's bill is many more cubic metres than 7.6% of a small flat's.
  • The effect in m³ depends on each household's level: its band, size and month.
  • So compute it for every household, then average: an average marginal effect.

This is the general lesson of the course: coefficients are on the model's scale; questions are on the reader's scale. Day 2 does the same for probabilities and counts.

Lab 1.4, step 3

An average marginal effect: predict, add one, predict again, average

1 · Predicteach bill as it is, in m³
→
2 · Changethe same bill with one more person
→
3 · Differsubtract, bill by bill
→
4 · Averageover the bills of each band
On the m³ scale. Each prediction is exp(prediction + σ²/2), the mean back-transform of session 3.

The average is over the households actually billed, so the result describes them, with their own mix of sizes and months. A marginal effect "at the mean" plugs in one average household instead and can differ; say which you report.

Lab 1.4, step 4

The interval comes from redoing the average under 1,000 plausible coefficient vectors

  • Draw coefficient vectors from their estimated sampling distribution.
  • Recompute the average marginal effect for each draw.
  • The 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles are the 95% interval.
U.contrast(f, b, "household_size",
           low=lambda x: x, high=lambda x: x + 1,
           scale="m3", by="tariff_band")

This is the course-provided function. The same idea is behind the delta method that marginaleffects uses by default; the stretch item compares them on the log scale, where both give the band slopes. In R: marginaleffects::avg_comparisons.

Lab 1.4, step 5

One more person goes with 1.6 m³ more on social and 5.0 m³ more on villa

Average marginal effect of household size by band, m³ a month, with 95% intervals. Lab 1.4, 40,000 bills.

Social 1.59 (1.52 to 1.67), standard 2.33 (2.27 to 2.39), villa 5.03 (4.84 to 5.20). This table, matched to the reference within 0.05, is the evidence of LO1. The ratio villa to social is above three, while the percentages differ by less than two to one.

The sentence

Write the effect as a comparison of households, not as what a tariff does

Supported

"Among billed villa households, one more person goes with about 5.0 m³ more a month (95% interval 4.8 to 5.2)."

Not supported

"The social tariff halves the water each extra person uses."

The course rule. Bands are tied to income and housing. No coefficient is presented as a tariff's effect on poorer households.

Return to claim 1 of lab 1.1. The second card turns a difference between groups of households into a claim about what the tariff does to families. Ask the room to rewrite it: "Social-tariff households with one more person use about 1.6 m³ more a month; villa households about 5.0."
